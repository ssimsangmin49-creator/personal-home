The problem is that slogans built around words such as *End* or *Zero*—words that can evade the very essence of the problem—may actually become obstacles to the spread of practical alternatives. Furthermore, unlike ordinary resources, plastic is something for which even the prefix *Re-* may serve to dilute public awareness of the problem. I believe we should be more restrained in our use of words such as *Reuse* and *Reduce* when discussing plastic. We must not allow such words to be perceived as adequate remedies for a problem in which the quantity of the material causing it continues to grow exponentially. Consider plastic drinking straws. We could campaign tirelessly, day after day, throughout an entire year to reduce their use, and the resulting reduction might still amount to less than the plastic waste discarded in China in a single day. I have no objection to social movements that begin by doing whatever is immediately possible. I would simply like to point out that many of the people and organizations leading these campaigns are not based in South Asia.

There is another issue we need to address: Waste-to-Energy (WtE), which deals with enormous quantities of polymer-based waste, including plastic waste, under the banner of *converting waste into energy*. Simply reverse the words. **We burn waste in order to produce energy.** In the East, we have an expression for this: *putting the cart before the horse*—confusing the means with the end. Pyrolysis is no different. If recovering oil from such materials were commercially viable on its own merits, the petroleum refining industry would surely have found ways to extract every last economically recoverable drop during the refining process. And yet we are now proposing to melt down plastics—products made from petroleum-derived materials—and extract oil that even the refining industry supposedly left behind? The truly troubling issue is that WtE and pyrolysis have, until now, been treated as virtually the only two alternatives available to humanity.

More than 3,000 WtE facilities are already operating around the world, most of them concentrated in North America, Europe, China, and Japan. At least these facilities thermally treat municipal solid waste (MSW) generated in and around major cities. In that sense, the cities are protected. Cities with WtE facilities are, to some extent, protected from their own waste. Yet even with these facilities, the proportion of total waste—including ordinary municipal waste—treated in this way remains below 30%. And this is where I wish to raise a question. **What becomes of the waste generated by regions without WtE facilities, or by cities and countries that simply cannot afford to build them?** Consider Jakarta, the capital of Indonesia. Repeated efforts have been made to establish WtE facilities there, only for projects to encounter serious difficulties or collapse because of financing constraints. The composition of waste in tropical regions has also played a role. So has the rising capital cost per ton of treatment capacity, which can increase beyond original projections between the planning stage and the time financing is finally arranged.

Meanwhile, our oceans are becoming seriously polluted by floating plastic debris and microplastics. The presence of microplastics in fish that eventually reach our dinner tables is an increasingly troubling concern. Even so, extraordinary efforts continue. People collect floating plastic waste from the sea, install barriers to intercept waste before it reaches the ocean, and devote themselves to preventing further pollution. They deserve our wholehearted applause.

Producers of plastic raw materials and manufacturers of plastic products pay various environmental charges and fees to governments. Then what about consumers? In principle, people should take responsibility for the waste they generate. Major cities, at least, have WtE facilities or comparable systems to deal with their waste. But the real difficulty lies with smaller cities, island communities, and public administrations in less-developed countries. No. Let me correct myself. **The real problem is that our capacity to treat plastic waste cannot keep pace with the volume being produced and sold.** And when people are already struggling to make a living, how much time, money, or attention can they afford to devote to the waste they throw away?

Even setting South Asia aside, anyone who has traveled through Southeast Asia will understand what I mean. In many of these countries, recycling seems to have surprisingly little relevance to the plastic waste problem I am describing. Not because recyclable plastics are necessarily ignored. Quite the opposite. You can hardly find them. If a discarded plastic item has monetary value, someone will collect it, wash it if necessary, and sell it. Then what remains? Plastic bags, Styrofoam, packaging materials, worn-out shoes, colored plastic containers, heavily contaminated plastics, and countless other forms of polymer-based waste that cannot find a place in the recycling economy. These materials continue to accumulate, sometimes to a scale that seems to rival the living spaces of the communities around them, contaminating their surroundings as they grow. It pains me to see how closely poverty and the accumulation of non-recyclable plastic waste appear to go hand in hand.

Even South Korea, which began centralizing its waste-treatment systems relatively early, is no exception. Following the war in the early 1950s, rapid urbanization and industrialization widened the economic gap between urban and non-urban areas, bringing with it unequal capacities to manage waste. Marine waste, in particular, sometimes seems to have been all but abandoned. To its credit, Korea does enforce waste separation and takes a relatively strict approach to illegal dumping.

This has become a rather long piece, and I have ventured into subjects that some may consider too broad or too contentious. Perhaps that is a kind of privilege enjoyed by someone who dares to propose an alternative. Most of the issues I have raised are, after all, already matters of public discussion. If I had the ability, I would like to go further—to examine the true nature of the plastic waste problem through my own uncompromising, perhaps even mercilessly sharp, powers of discernment. But this is a piece written for my personal homepage.
